\begin{abstract}
We look at how heat spreads along a thin rod whose ends are held at fixed
temperatures, and at how a simple numerical scheme reproduces it.
\end{abstract}

\input{sections/intro}

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}
The temperature $u(x,t)$ obeys the heat equation
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}, \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha > 0$ is the thermal diffusivity. The ends are held at fixed temperatures:
\begin{align}
  u(0,t) &= T_0, \label{eq:bc0} \\
  u(L,t) &= T_1. \label{eq:bc1}
\end{align}
As $t \to \infty$ the temperature settles to the steady state
\begin{equation}
  u_s(x) = T_0 + (T_1 - T_0)\frac{x}{L}. \label{eq:steady}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Maximum principle]\label{thm:max}
The largest and smallest temperatures are reached at $t = 0$ or at the ends of the rod.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At an interior maximum $u_{xx} \le 0$, so by \eqref{eq:heat} $u$ cannot grow there.
\end{proof}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\section{Discussion}
The explicit scheme is simple, but its time step must shrink like $\Delta x^2$,
which makes fine grids expensive. Implicit schemes such as Crank--Nicolson
\cite{crank1975} avoid this limit:\footnote{At the cost of solving a tridiagonal system at every step.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item they are stable for every $r > 0$, and
  \item they are second-order accurate in both $\Delta t$ and $\Delta x$.
\end{itemize}
